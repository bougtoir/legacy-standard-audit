# TFSC novelty assessment

## Review basis

This assessment uses 32 DOI-verified publications, including ten articles in
*Technological Forecasting and Social Change* (TFSC), spanning standards
economics, path dependence, technological trajectories, socio-technical
transitions, infrastructure lock-in, digital innovation, standards governance,
and switching behavior. Exact Crossref records and checksums are registered in
`SOURCE_REGISTRY.csv`; the comparison fields are in
`TFSC_prior_art_matrix.csv`.

The review is deliberately bounded. It establishes the nearest theoretical and
methodological neighborhoods; it is not a claim to have exhaustively enumerated
every standards case study.

## What prior work already establishes

1. **Persistence need not imply error.** Increasing returns, compatibility,
   installed bases, network externalities, institutional feedback, and
   switching costs can make persistence rational even when an alternative has
   attractive technical properties.
2. **Technological change is path-dependent.** Paradigms, trajectories,
   dominant designs, architectural innovation, and organizational capabilities
   constrain which alternatives become visible and feasible.
3. **Standards are both enabling and constraining.** They support coordination,
   interoperability, quality, knowledge codification, and innovation, while
   sometimes narrowing variety or entrenching an architecture.
4. **Transitions are socio-technical.** Infrastructure, policy, organizations,
   users, markets, and technology co-evolve; technical replacement alone is an
   incomplete account.
5. **Revision and escape are governance problems.** TFSC research already
   addresses standards battles, lock-in and break-out, transition governance,
   real-time foresight, policy implementation, and switching behavior.

Accordingly, the manuscript cannot credibly claim that it discovered lock-in,
that old standards may become mismatched, that technology alters institutions,
or that switching costs matter.

## Narrowest defensible novelty

The defensible contribution is a **pre-specified, falsifiable measurement and
governance protocol** that combines concepts previously treated separately:

1. reconstruct the objective, information set, feasible set, and coordination
   conditions under which an incumbent standard was adopted or locked in;
2. identify a specific technological mechanism that changes one or more of
   those conditions;
3. estimate the incumbent's performance against a contemporary frictionless
   optimum under explicitly stated objectives and uncertainty;
4. distinguish static regret from a transition-adjusted optimum that includes
   switching, safety, regulatory, compatibility, and network costs;
5. retain null and negative-control cases selected before results are known;
6. classify each case as material mismatch, small or uncertain mismatch, no
   mismatch, or insufficient evidence; and
7. derive revision governance from the source of persistence rather than from
   standard age.

The novelty is therefore mainly **operational and methodological synthesis**.
Its empirical strength will depend on whether the frozen cases can be
reconstructed with sufficiently comparable public evidence. The project should
not describe the framework as a new general theory until the empirical
falsification tests support that stronger claim.

## TFSC-specific positioning

The closest TFSC streams cover:

- standards and innovation;
- standards battles and dominance;
- standards-induced path dependence;
- lock-in and escape from technological trajectories;
- socio-technical transition patterns and governance;
- real-time foresight; and
- switching to disruptive technologies.

The proposed paper differs by making the **post-adoption standard itself** the
unit of audit and by requiring a common sequence of historical reconstruction,
technological constraint change, frictionless regret, transition-adjusted
regret, and falsification. This places the paper primarily in the Technology
Governance and Public Policy bureau, with a secondary fit to Impact and
Evaluation of Technology.

## Claims that must be avoided

- “Legacy standards are obsolete” or “old standards are inefficient.”
- “Technology causes standards to become inefficient” from a convenience
  sample.
- “The framework is the first analysis of lock-in, path dependence, standards
  revision, or transition costs.”
- A universal cross-domain regret index that aggregates incomparable outcomes.
- Broad social-welfare conclusions from narrow engineering objectives.
- Treating a positive frictionless gap as evidence that immediate replacement
  is desirable.
- Treating the 1950–1980 period as causal rather than historically descriptive.
- Presenting a case's apparent original rationale as verified when primary or
  authoritative evidence is unavailable.

## Falsifiable contribution test

The contribution weakens materially if:

- incumbent configurations generally remain within current-optimum
  uncertainty;
- plausible objectives eliminate estimated regret;
- transition costs dominate in nearly all cases;
- technology-mediated cases do not differ descriptively from controls;
- one flagship case determines the synthesis;
- historical objectives cannot be reconstructed; or
- case outcomes are too heterogeneous for any common inference beyond the
  protocol itself.

Those findings remain publishable only if the manuscript is reframed as a
negative audit showing why simple claims about obsolete standards fail.
